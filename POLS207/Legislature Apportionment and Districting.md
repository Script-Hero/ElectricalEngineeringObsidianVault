## Congressional Redistricting & Representation: Key Concepts

---

### The Impact of Reapportionment
Reapportionment is the process of **redistributing the 435 seats in the U.S. House of Representatives** among the states after every 10-year census. States that gain population may receive more seats, while those with stagnant or declining populations may lose seats. This shift can affect the **balance of political power** at both state and national levels.

---

### Districting: Partisan and Incumbent Gerrymandering
**Gerrymandering** is the manipulation of district boundaries to benefit specific political interests.

- **Partisan Gerrymandering**: Drawing districts to favor one political party over another.
- **Incumbent Gerrymandering**: Designing districts to protect current officeholders, often by creating “safe” seats with favorable voter demographics.

These practices can reduce electoral competition and distort representation.

---

### The Seats-Votes Relationship
This refers to the connection (or mismatch) between the **percentage of votes** a party receives and the **percentage of seats** it wins. Due to districting, a party might win **fewer or more seats** than its vote share would suggest—often as a result of gerrymandering or how votes are geographically distributed.

---

### Affirmative Racial Gerrymandering
This involves drawing districts to **increase representation for racial or ethnic minorities**, often to comply with the Voting Rights Act. While intended to ensure fair representation, it can be controversial if seen as prioritizing race over other criteria or creating oddly shaped districts.

---

### Multimember Districts
These are districts that elect **more than one representative**. They are less common in federal elections but sometimes used in state legislatures. Multimember districts can increase proportional representation but may also dilute minority voting strength or reduce accountability.

---

### District Size
District size can refer to both **population size** and **geographic size**. In the House, each district must have roughly equal population ("one person, one vote"), but rural districts may be geographically large, while urban ones are compact. Size affects **representation, accessibility**, and **campaign strategy**.

---

### Who Draws the Lines?
Redistricting is usually done by:
- **State legislatures** (most common),
- **Independent or bipartisan commissions** (used in some states to reduce partisanship),
- Or a **court**, if other methods fail.

Who draws the lines significantly impacts how fair or partisan the resulting maps are.

---

### How Often to Redistrict?
Redistricting occurs **every 10 years**, after the census. This ensures districts reflect population changes. However, some states allow redistricting **more frequently**, which can open the door to more frequent gerrymandering unless restricted by law.
