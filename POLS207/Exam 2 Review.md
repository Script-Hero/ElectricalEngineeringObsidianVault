# Detailed Study Guide

---

## 1. How do most people participate in the political system?

Most people participate through **voting in elections**, but participation also includes activities like:

- Joining political parties or interest groups
- Attending public meetings or protests
- Volunteering for campaigns
- Donating to campaigns or candidates
- Writing to elected officials

---

## 2. What is a rational voter?

A rational voter makes decisions based on a cost-benefit analysis, voting only if the perceived **benefit** (e.g., influencing policy, civic duty) outweighs the **cost** (time, effort, inconvenience). Rational voters are often informed on issues and candidates.

---

## 3. Know who votes and who does not

**People who typically vote:**
- Older individuals
- Higher-income earners
- Higher education levels
- Homeowners
- Those with strong political or community connections

**People who typically do not vote:**
- Young adults (18-25)
- Lower-income individuals
- Lower education levels
- Renters or frequent movers
- Those feeling disconnected from the political process

---

## 4. Why do some states have higher voter turnout?

Factors contributing to higher voter turnout include:
- Easier voter registration processes
- Competitive elections or close races
- Higher levels of education and income
- Cultural emphasis on civic participation
- Convenient voting practices (early voting, mail-in voting, same-day registration)

---

## 5. How could voter turnout be increased?

- Simplify voter registration (automatic or same-day registration)
- Implement early or mail-in voting
- Make Election Day a holiday or weekend voting
- Enhance voter education and engagement
- Reduce barriers (e.g., voter ID laws)

---

## 6. Who or what determines voter qualifications?

- Voter qualifications are primarily determined by **state governments**.
- Federal laws and constitutional amendments limit how states can restrict voting rights.

---

## 7. Know the amendments to the U.S. Constitution that concern voting

- **15th Amendment**: Voting cannot be denied based on race or color.
- **17th Amendment**: Direct election of U.S. senators by voters.
- **19th Amendment**: Women’s right to vote.
- **24th Amendment**: Eliminated poll taxes.
- **26th Amendment**: Voting age lowered to 18.

---

## 8. Know the responsible party model

A model where political parties offer clear, distinct policy choices to voters, campaign based on these platforms, and once elected, implement those policies. Voters then judge the party based on performance at the next election.

---

## 9. Know the different types of primaries

- **Open Primary**: Any voter can vote in any party primary.
- **Closed Primary**: Only registered party members can vote.
- **Semi-closed**: Party members and independents can participate.
- **Runoff Primary**: If no candidate achieves majority, the top two candidates compete in a runoff.

---

## 10. Know the different types of elections

- **General Election**: Selects who holds office.
- **Primary Election**: Selects party candidates for the general election.
- **Initiative**: Voters propose and vote directly on legislation.
- **Recall**: Voters can remove elected officials before their term ends.
- **Referendum**: Voters directly vote on laws or constitutional amendments.

---

## 11. What do the national party committees do?

- Coordinate national campaigns and conventions
- Raise funds for national and state-level campaigns
- Provide support for candidates
- Promote party platforms and policy agendas

---

## 12. When is negative campaigning successful?

Negative campaigning tends to be effective when:
- Information is credible and memorable
- It highlights significant weaknesses or controversies
- The electorate is undecided or uncertain about a candidate
- Used strategically in competitive elections

---

## 13. Know the following Supreme Court cases:

### a. **Baker v. Carr (1962)**  
- Established that federal courts can hear cases on legislative apportionment. Established "one person, one vote" principle in legislative districting.

### b. **Reynolds v. Sims (1964)**  
- Reinforced "one person, one vote" by requiring state legislative districts to have roughly equal populations.

---

## 14. Know the functions of legislatures

- Pass laws and policies
- Represent constituent interests
- Approve budgets and taxes
- Provide oversight of the executive branch
- Engage in constituent service

---

## 15. Why do incumbents win?

- Name recognition and visibility
- Easier access to campaign funding
- Established record of constituent services
- Gerrymandered or favorable districts
- Advantages of office (franking privileges, etc.)

---

## 16. What is apportionment?

Apportionment is the process of allocating legislative seats (such as U.S. House seats) among states based on population. It occurs every ten years after the census.

---

## 17. What is gerrymandering?

Drawing electoral district boundaries intentionally to benefit a specific political party or group. Types include:
- **Partisan Gerrymandering**
- **Incumbent Gerrymandering**
- **Racial Gerrymandering**

---

## 18. Know the different types of legislative sessions

- **Regular session**: Scheduled session occurring annually or biennially.
- **Special session**: Called outside regular schedule to address specific issues.
- **Interim session**: Periods between formal sessions where committees meet and discuss policy issues.

---

## 19. What do committees do?

- Review, amend, and filter legislation before chamber-wide votes
- Hold hearings and gather information on issues
- Conduct oversight of government agencies
- Provide specialized policy expertise

---

## 20. Who determines the membership of legislative committees?

Committee membership is usually determined by:
- Chamber leadership (Speaker, Senate President)
- Party leaders
- Seniority, member preferences, and expertise

---

## 21. What is pigeonholing?

When a committee chair or committee refuses to act on or consider a bill, effectively preventing it from advancing in the legislative process.

---

## 22. Know the requirements for election to the Texas State Legislature

- **House**: 21 years old, Texas resident 2 years, district resident 1 year.
- **Senate**: 26 years old, Texas resident 5 years, district resident 1 year.

---

## 23. How is legislative leadership chosen?

- **Chamber members typically elect leaders** (e.g., Speaker in House, President in Senate).
- Leadership elections influenced by majority party, seniority, and internal caucus agreements.

---

## 24. How long is the legislative session in Texas?

- Regular sessions last **140 days**, every two years (biennially) in odd-numbered years.
- Special sessions may be called by the governor, lasting up to 30 days each.

---

## 25. Know the principal-agent model discussed in class

A model describing the relationship between a "principal" (voters) who delegates authority to an "agent" (elected representatives). Issues arise from information asymmetry, conflicting interests, and challenges ensuring accountability of elected officials to voters.
